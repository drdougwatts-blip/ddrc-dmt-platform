01
Decompression Illness
Pathophysiology, types, and recognition

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
DCI Pathophysiology
Inert gas (usually nitrogen) dissolves in tissues under pressure (Henry's Law)
On decompression, dissolved gas comes out of solution
If decompression is too fast, gas forms bubbles in tissues and blood
Bubbles cause mechanical obstruction, endothelial damage, and inflammatory response
Location of bubbles determines the clinical presentation
Individual susceptibility varies: same dive profile, different outcomes between divers

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Type I vs Type II DCI

Type I (musculoskeletal / cutaneous)
Type II (neurological / cardiopulmonary)
Joint pain (the bends): most common presentation
Usually shoulders, elbows, knees
Skin: itching, rash, marbling (cutis marmorata)
Lymphatic: localised swelling
Not immediately life-threatening but needs treatment
Spinal cord: weakness, numbness, paraplegia, bladder dysfunction
Cerebral: confusion, visual disturbance, speech problems, hemiplegia
Vestibular: vertigo, nausea, hearing loss (the staggers)
Cardiopulmonary: the chokes (cough, chest pain, dyspnoea)
Medical emergency: requires urgent recompression

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
Arterial Gas Embolism (AGE)
Mechanism: pulmonary barotrauma on ascent, gas enters pulmonary veins
Gas travels to cerebral or coronary circulation
Presentation: sudden onset, usually within minutes of surfacing
Neurological: sudden loss of consciousness, seizure, focal deficit
Cardiac: arrhythmia, chest pain, cardiac arrest
Distinguishing AGE from neurological DCI can be difficult and is often academic
Treatment is the same: urgent recompression

02
Differential Diagnosis and Treatment

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
DCI vs Other Conditions

Similar presentations
Practical approach
Stroke: sudden neuro deficit. No diving history needed.
Head injury: may co-exist with a diving incident
Inner ear barotrauma: vertigo, hearing loss (no bubbles)
Musculoskeletal injury: joint pain from physical strain
Any neurological symptom after diving: assume DCI until proven otherwise
High-flow oxygen immediately (100%, non-rebreather mask)
Full neurological examination and documentation
Contact the physician / diving medicine specialist
Let the expert decide the treatment pathway

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
Therapeutic Recompression
Principle: recompress the patient to reduce bubble size and drive gas back into solution
RN62 (USN Table 6): most commonly used treatment table for DCI
Initial compression to 18 msw (2.8 ATA) on 100% O2 with air breaks
Extensions possible if symptoms not resolving
RN67 (USN Table 6A): deeper table (50 msw) for severe or non-responsive cases
Comex tables: used in some saturation scenarios
Table selection is a physician decision, not a DMT decision
The DMT's role: recognise DCI, start O2, communicate findings, prepare for recompression

03
Return to Diving and Travel After DCI

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Current Guidance
Return to diving after DCI: physician decision based on severity and resolution
Type I DCI: typically minimum 2-4 weeks with medical clearance
Type II DCI: typically minimum 4-6 weeks, may require specialist assessment
Residual symptoms may permanently disqualify from diving
Flying after diving: current DAN/UHMS guidance (typically 12-24 hours minimum)
Flying after treatment for DCI: much longer delay required (typically 72 hours minimum)
The DMT advises divers on these guidelines and ensures physician involvement

04
O2 Toxicity, Barotrauma, and Thermal Stress Updates

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Oxygen Toxicity: Key Updates

CNS toxicity
Pulmonary toxicity
ppO2 threshold: approximately 1.6 bar (variable between individuals)
VENTID-C: vision, ears, nausea, twitching, irritability, dizziness, convulsions
Convulsion management: protect, do not restrain, reduce ppO2
In-chamber seizure: switch off BIBS O2, maintain depth
Prolonged ppO2 > 0.5 bar
Chest tightness, cough, reduced vital capacity
Managed with air breaks during treatment tables
UPTD tracking for cumulative exposure
Relevant during extended recompression treatments

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Barotrauma and Thermal Stress: Management Update
Barotrauma principles unchanged: Boyle's Law, gas-filled spaces, pressure change
Tension pneumothorax: immediate decompression remains the priority
Thermal stress update: hypothermia and hyperthermia management per current guidelines
Hot water suit failures in saturation: rapid heat loss, core temperature monitoring
Hyperthermia in tropical operations: deck work, inadequate hydration
Current evidence on cold water immersion survival times and rescue techniques

Key Takeaways
DCI: recognise the presentation, start high-flow O2, full neuro exam, contact physician
Type I vs Type II: the distinction guides treatment urgency. Type II is a medical emergency.
AGE: sudden onset on surfacing. Same treatment pathway as neurological DCI.
Recompression: RN62 is the workhorse. Table selection is a physician decision.
Return to diving and flying after DCI: follow current guidance, involve the physician
Everything in this session feeds into the in-person neuro exam and assessed DCI scenario
