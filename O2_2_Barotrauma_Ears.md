01
Squeeze
Mask, suit, sinus, and general principles

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Types of Squeeze

Mask and suit
Sinus squeeze
Mask squeeze: negative pressure, periorbital bruising, subconjunctival haemorrhage
Prevention: equalise mask on descent
Suit squeeze: skin folds trapped, linear bruising
Prevention: inflate suit adequately on descent
Both usually self-limiting
Frontal and maxillary sinuses: air-filled cavities
On descent: negative pressure causes pain, mucosal swelling
Epistaxis (nosebleed) is common
Referred pain can mimic toothache
If symptoms persist: refer for specialist assessment
Prevention: avoid diving with upper respiratory infection

02
Ear Barotrauma
Middle ear, inner ear, and the critical distinction

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
Middle Ear Barotrauma
Most common barotrauma in diving
Eustachian tube fails to open on descent: pressure differential across the eardrum
Progressive: pain, hearing loss, tympanic membrane rupture
Teed classification: Grade I (injection) through Grade V (perforation)
Vertigo may occur if cold water enters through a perforation
Management: stop descent, ascend gently, no further diving until cleared
Otoscopic examination will be practised in-person (Day 3)

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
Inner Ear Barotrauma

Mechanism
Recognition and urgency
Forceful equalisation against a blocked Eustachian tube
Pressure wave transmitted to round or oval window
Window rupture: perilymph fistula
Can occur on descent or ascent
Sudden sensorineural hearing loss
Vertigo (often severe and persistent)
Tinnitus
This is an urgent specialist referral
Critical distinction from middle ear injury: inner ear = specialist, same day

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->

Middle ear barotrauma is common and usually self-limiting.
Inner ear barotrauma requires urgent specialist referral.
Getting this distinction right matters. If in doubt, assume inner ear and refer.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
Alternobaric Vertigo
Asymmetric equalisation between the two ears
One middle ear equalises, the other does not
Unequal pressure stimulates the vestibular system: vertigo
Can occur on ascent (reverse squeeze) or descent
Usually brief and self-limiting once pressures equalise
Risk: disorientation underwater, panic, loss of buoyancy control
Management: stop, hold depth, wait for it to pass. Ascend slowly if it does not resolve.

03
Ear Infections and Saturation Hygiene

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Ear Infections in Diving

Otitis externa
Otitis media
Very common in saturation: humidity, warmth, earphone use
Signs: pain on ear tug, swelling of canal, discharge
First-line: antibiotic/steroid ear drops
Prevention: drying after washing, prophylactic drops
Ear hygiene protocols should be in place for every sat run
Less common but more significant
Middle ear infection behind the drum
Pain, hearing loss, possibly fever
May need oral antibiotics (physician decision)
Diving fitness: cannot dive with active otitis media
DMT advises the physician, physician makes fitness decision

04
Lung Overexpansion Injuries
Pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, and arterial gas embolism

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Mechanism
Gas trapped in the lungs expands on ascent (Boyle's Law)
Causes: breath-holding on ascent, gas trapping from airway disease
Can occur with very small pressure changes (even in a swimming pool depth)
The expanding gas can rupture alveoli and track to different locations:
Into the pleural space: pneumothorax
Into the mediastinum: pneumomediastinum
Into subcutaneous tissues: subcutaneous emphysema
Into pulmonary veins and to the brain: arterial gas embolism (AGE)

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Pneumothorax

Simple pneumothorax
Tension pneumothorax
Air in pleural space, lung partially collapsed
Chest pain, dyspnoea, reduced breath sounds on affected side
Management: oxygen, monitor, evacuate
One-way valve: air enters pleural space but cannot escape
Lung fully collapsed, mediastinal shift, cardiovascular compromise
Signs: tracheal deviation, absent breath sounds, hypotension, distended neck veins
Life-threatening emergency: requires immediate decompression
Chest drain: theory today, demonstration in-person

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Pneumomediastinum, Emphysema, and AGE Introduction
Pneumomediastinum: air in the mediastinum. Chest pain, voice change, subcutaneous crepitus in neck.
Subcutaneous emphysema: air under the skin. Crackling sensation on palpation. Usually self-limiting.
Arterial gas embolism: air enters pulmonary veins, travels to brain/coronary arteries
AGE presentation: sudden neurological deficit immediately on surfacing
AGE is covered in detail in the DCI module (Thursday shared session)
Key point: lung overpressure can cause both local (chest) and systemic (brain) injury

05
Skin and Eye Injuries

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
Common Presentations
Suit squeeze injuries: linear bruising from skin folds under neoprene
Marine life contact: coral cuts (infected easily), jellyfish stings
Cuts and abrasions: common in the offshore working environment
Eyes: mask squeeze (subconjunctival haemorrhage), chemical exposure, foreign bodies, welding flash
Management: irrigate eyes with clean water, remove foreign bodies if superficial
Wound care under pressure: dressings in a confined, humid chamber environment
When to refer: penetrating eye injury, chemical burns, infection not responding to first aid

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
Key Takeaways
All barotrauma is Boyle's Law: gas-filled spaces and pressure change
Ear barotrauma: distinguish middle ear (common, self-limiting) from inner ear (urgent referral)
Ear infections in saturation: prevention through hygiene protocols is better than treatment
Lung overexpansion: pneumothorax (especially tension) is a life-threatening emergency
AGE: sudden neuro deficit on surfacing. Covered in full in the DCI module.
Ear examination and chest drain demonstration are practised in-person
