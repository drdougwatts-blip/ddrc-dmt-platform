01
Pressure Fundamentals
Units, ambient pressure, and Boyle's Law

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Pressure at Depth
Atmospheric pressure at surface: 1 bar (1 ATA)
Pressure increases by 1 bar for every 10 msw
Absolute pressure = gauge pressure + atmospheric
At 30 msw: absolute pressure = 4 bar (4 ATA)
Units: bar, ATA, msw, fsw (know the equivalences, use one system)

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Boyle's Law

The law
Clinical relevance
At constant temperature: P1 x V1 = P2 x V2
Pressure up = volume down (and vice versa)
Applies to every gas-filled space in the body
Applies to every gas-filled space in equipment
Ear squeeze on descent: middle ear volume shrinks
Lung overexpansion on ascent: gas volume increases
Pneumothorax worsens on ascent
Mask squeeze: negative pressure in mask space

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->

Worked Example: Boyle's Law
A diver develops a pneumothorax at 30 metres (4 ATA). The trapped gas volume is 500 ml. If the diver ascends to the surface (1 ATA) without treatment, what volume will the gas occupy?
Discussion Points
Apply P1 x V1 = P2 x V2
4 x 500 = 1 x V2
V2 = 2000 ml
A 500 ml pneumothorax becomes 2 litres at the surface: life-threatening

02
Gas Mixtures and Partial Pressures
Dalton's Law and toxicity thresholds

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
Dalton's Law
Total pressure = sum of partial pressures of each gas
Partial pressure = fraction of gas x absolute pressure
Air: 21% O2, 79% N2 (simplified)
At surface: ppO2 = 0.21 bar, ppN2 = 0.79 bar
At 30 msw (4 ATA): ppO2 = 0.84 bar, ppN2 = 3.16 bar
Toxicity depends on partial pressure, not percentage

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
Partial Pressure Thresholds

Oxygen
Other gases
CNS toxicity risk: ppO2 above 1.6 bar
Pulmonary toxicity: prolonged ppO2 above 0.5 bar
Treatment tables: ppO2 up to 2.8 bar (controlled)
Hypoxia: ppO2 below 0.16 bar
Nitrogen narcosis: ppN2 above ~3.2 bar (roughly 30 msw on air)
CO2 toxicity: ppCO2 above 0.05 bar
CO poisoning: even very low fractions become toxic at depth
Threshold = fraction x depth: small contamination, big problem

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->

Calculation Exercise
A diver breathes air at 50 msw (6 ATA). Calculate: (1) the partial pressure of oxygen, (2) the partial pressure of nitrogen, and (3) whether either exceeds toxicity thresholds.
Discussion Points
ppO2 = 0.21 x 6 = 1.26 bar (approaching CNS toxicity threshold)
ppN2 = 0.79 x 6 = 4.74 bar (significant narcosis expected)
Both are clinically relevant at this depth

03
Gas Solubility and Bubbles
Henry's Law and the basis of decompression illness

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
Henry's Law
Gas dissolves in liquid in proportion to the partial pressure above it
Higher pressure (deeper depth) = more gas dissolved in tissues
On ascent, pressure drops and gas comes out of solution
If ascent is controlled: gas eliminated safely through lungs
If ascent is too fast: gas forms bubbles in tissues and blood
This is the mechanism of decompression illness

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Tissue Loading: A Simplified Model
Different tissues absorb and release gas at different rates
Fast tissues (blood, brain): load and unload quickly
Slow tissues (fat, cartilage): load and unload slowly
Decompression schedules account for these different rates
Saturation: all tissues fully loaded after prolonged exposure at depth
The DCI module covers pathophysiology in full detail

Thermal Physics and Gas Density
Heat loss and work of breathing

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Heat Loss and Gas Density

Thermal physics
Gas density at depth
Heat loss: conduction, convection, radiation, evaporation
Water conducts heat 25x faster than air
Helium conducts heat 6x faster than air
Saturation divers lose heat rapidly without active heating
Gas becomes denser at higher pressure
Denser gas = harder to breathe (increased work of breathing)
Relevant at deep depths on air or heavy mixes
Helium is less dense: used for deep diving
