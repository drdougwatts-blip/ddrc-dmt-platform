### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Airway and Breathing

A: Airway
B: Breathing
Is the patient talking? If yes, airway is open (for now).
Causes of obstruction: tongue, blood, vomit, swelling
Interventions: positioning, suction, adjuncts (OPA, NPA, i-gel)
Fix before moving to B
Look: chest movement, symmetry, rate, effort
Listen: air entry, added sounds
Feel: tracheal position, subcutaneous emphysema
Life threats: tension pneumothorax, flail chest
Count the respiratory rate. Give oxygen early.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Circulation, Disability, and Exposure

C and D
E: Exposure
C: Pulse, skin colour, capillary refill, bleeding control
Catastrophic haemorrhage: tourniquet if limb, direct pressure if trunk
D: AVPU or GCS, pupils, blood glucose
Lateralising signs: one side different from the other
Fully examine: front and back, head to toe
Remove clothing as needed (maintain dignity)
Temperature: measure it, do not guess
Log roll if spinal injury suspected
Then cover the patient: prevent heat loss

Secondary Survey and History

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
Secondary Survey
Only after all life threats are managed in the primary survey
Head-to-toe systematic examination: scalp, face, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, limbs, back
Look, feel, move (where appropriate and not contraindicated)
AMPLE history: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events
Document all findings: this feeds into the DMAC 01 report
Re-assessment: return to primary survey if the patient deteriorates at any point

03
On-Site Casualty Management

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
Management Principles Review
Safety: scene safety, PPE. You are no use as a second casualty.
Positioning: recovery position (unconscious, breathing), sitting upright (breathing difficulty), legs elevated (shock)
Oxygen: give it when there is any clinical concern. High flow via reservoir mask.
Warmth: offshore patients lose heat rapidly. Blankets, shelter, warm IV fluids.
Spinal precautions: if mechanism suggests spinal injury, immobilise until cleared
Current evidence has shifted away from routine spinal immobilisation for all trauma. Selective approach based on mechanism and symptoms.
Reassurance: a conscious patient is frightened. Talk to them. Explain what you are doing.

04
Patient Movement in Diving Operations

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Within the Dive System

Bell to chamber transfer
Chamber to vessel medical facility
Small hatches, limited space, difficult manoeuvring
Patient may be on a stretcher or spinal board
Requires teamwork and planning before starting the move
Maintain monitoring throughout the transfer
May require decompression first (cannot move a pressurised patient to surface)
If DCI: the patient stays under pressure. Treatment in the chamber.
Equipment restrictions: what fits through the hatch?
Hyperbaric rescue unit (HRU): for emergency transfer under pressure

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
Helicopter Transfer Considerations
Altitude: even low-altitude flights reduce ambient pressure. Relevant for DCI and pneumothorax.
Many helicopter services will fly at sea level or minimal altitude for diving casualties
Noise and vibration: monitoring is difficult. Secure all equipment.
Limited space: the DMT may or may not escort the patient
If escorting: prepare a written handover in case you become unwell or the situation changes
Handover to the receiving medical team: use DMAC 01 structure, be concise
Coordinate timing with the supervisor and vessel operations

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->

Case Discussion
A diver is recovered from the bell after a routine bell run at 60 msw in saturation. On arrival in the main chamber he is complaining of severe abdominal pain and is pale and sweating. He vomited once in the bell. His bellman reports he was fine during the dive but complained of pain during the bell recovery.
Discussion Points
Walk through ABCDE for this patient in the chamber environment
What are the differential diagnoses?
What can you do in the chamber? What can you not do?
When and how do you contact the physician?

Key Takeaways
ABCDE: the framework for every patient. Fix life threats in order. Do not skip steps.
Secondary survey only after the primary survey is clear
Positioning, oxygen, warmth, reassurance: the fundamentals of on-site care
Moving patients in a dive system requires planning, teamwork, and maintained monitoring
Helicopter transfer: altitude matters for diving casualties. Communicate restrictions.
