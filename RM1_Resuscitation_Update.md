### Notes:

Adult BLS Algorithm: Current Guidance
Unresponsive and not breathing normally: call for help, start CPR
30 compressions : 2 ventilations (unchanged)
Compression rate: 100-120 per minute
Compression depth: 5-6 cm in an adult
Full chest recoil between compressions
Minimise interruptions: aim for <10 seconds between last compression and first post-pause compression
Hands-only CPR is acceptable if rescuer unable or unwilling to ventilate

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Compression Quality: What Matters

The fundamentals
What has changed recently
Rate 100-120/min (use a metronome or count)
Depth 5-6 cm (push hard)
Full recoil (let the chest come back up fully)
Minimal interruptions (keep compressing)
Switch compressor every 2 minutes (fatigue degrades quality)
Check for specific ERC/ILCOR guideline updates before delivery
Emphasis on compression quality over all other interventions
Early defibrillation emphasis (AED within 3-5 minutes)
Recognition of cardiac arrest: agonal breathing is not normal breathing
Update your slides to reflect the most current published guidance

02
AED Use
Integration, safety, and special considerations

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
AED in the BLS Algorithm
Switch on the AED. Follow voice prompts. Attach pads.
Pad placement: right clavicle and left mid-axillary line
Ensure nobody is touching the patient during analysis and shock delivery
Resume CPR immediately after shock (or if no shock advised)
Continue following AED prompts every 2 minutes
Wet environments: dry the chest around the pad sites. AEDs are safe in rain/wet decks.
Hyperbaric AED use: discuss any specific guidance for your equipment
Paediatric pads: use adult pads if paediatric not available (better than no AED)

### Notes:

Airway Management Overview

Basic airway interventions
Supraglottic airway developments
Head tilt chin lift (if no spinal concern)
Jaw thrust (if spinal injury suspected)
Suction for blood, vomit, secretions
OPA and NPA sizing and insertion
i-gel: increasingly standard for non-intubation trained rescuers
No inflation required (unlike classic LMA)
Correct sizing: important for effective seal
All practical airway skills are taught in-person Day 1

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
Choking Management
Partial obstruction: encourage coughing. Do not intervene if patient can cough effectively.
Complete obstruction (conscious): up to 5 back blows, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, alternate
Complete obstruction (unconscious): start CPR. Compressions may dislodge the object.
Check the mouth before each ventilation attempt
Large patients or pregnant patients: chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts

Special Circumstances
Drowning, hypothermia, and confined spaces

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
Special Resuscitation Scenarios

Drowning modifications
Hypothermic arrest
5 initial rescue breaths before starting compressions
Oxygenation is the priority (respiratory cause of arrest)
Vomiting is common: be prepared to manage the airway
Hypothermia often concurrent: see hypothermia protocol
Continue CPR regardless of apparent signs of death
Limit drug doses (drugs accumulate in cold patient)
Defibrillation may be ineffective below 30 degrees C
Rewarm before making decisions about cessation
Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Resuscitation in Confined Spaces
Bell or chamber: extremely limited space for CPR
External cardiac massage may need modified hand position or technique
Two-person CPR is difficult or impossible in some configurations
Prioritise compression quality even if you cannot achieve ideal positioning
AED use inside a chamber: confirm equipment is chamber-rated
Day 4 covers this in detail with the specialist instructor and actual chamber access

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
When to Start and When to Stop
Start: any patient in cardiac arrest unless clearly incompatible with life
Offshore: help is not coming quickly. You may resuscitate for a prolonged period.
Stopping: physician guidance. Do not stop unilaterally.
Factors: duration of arrest, response to treatment, reversible causes addressed
Futility: a difficult judgement. Always involve the physician in the decision.
Emotional impact on the rescuer: this is real. Support is available. Talk about it afterwards.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Key Takeaways
Compression quality is the single most important factor in survival
AED: switch on, follow prompts, shock early
Drowning: 5 rescue breaths first, then standard CPR
Hypothermia: do not give up. Continue CPR. Rewarm.
Confined spaces: prioritise compression quality with adapted technique
Stopping resuscitation: always with physician guidance, never alone
