Quick Revision: Partial Pressure
Partial pressure = fraction of gas x absolute pressure
Toxicity depends on partial pressure, not percentage of the gas
A gas that is harmless at the surface can be lethal at depth
A small contamination in the gas supply becomes a big problem under pressure
If you cannot calculate ppO2 at depth, the rest of this module will not make sense

01
Oxygen Toxicity
CNS and pulmonary manifestations

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
CNS Oxygen Toxicity (Paul Bert Effect)

Mechanism and thresholds
VENTID-C recognition tool
Occurs at ppO2 above approximately 1.6 bar
During treatment tables: ppO2 up to 2.8 bar (controlled exposure)
Individual susceptibility varies considerably
Onset may be sudden with no reliable warning signs
V: Vision disturbance (tunnel vision, blurring)
E: Ears (tinnitus, ringing)
N: Nausea
T: Twitching (facial muscles, lips, fingers)
I: Irritability, anxiety
D: Dizziness
C: Convulsions (may be the first and only sign)

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Managing an Oxygen Toxicity Convulsion
Protect the patient from injury: do not restrain
Remove from high ppO2 environment if possible (switch to air, reduce depth)
Maintain the airway once the seizure stops
In a chamber: switch off BIBS oxygen, maintain chamber depth
Do not attempt to ascend a convulsing diver
Recovery position after the seizure subsides
Contact the physician immediately

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
Pulmonary Oxygen Toxicity (Lorrain Smith Effect)
Occurs with prolonged exposure to ppO2 above 0.5 bar
Develops over hours to days, not minutes
Symptoms: chest tightness, cough, substernal pain, reduced vital capacity
Relevant during extended therapeutic recompression
Unit Pulmonary Toxicity Dose (UPTD): tracks cumulative exposure
Managed by air breaks during treatment table oxygen breathing

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
CO2 in Diving

Causes
Recognition and management
Skip breathing (deliberate breath holding to conserve gas)
Inadequate ventilation with increased work rate
Dead space in breathing apparatus
Scrubber failure in rebreather or saturation system
High CO2 in chamber atmosphere
Headache (often the first symptom)
Confusion, impaired judgement
Dyspnoea, increased respiratory rate
Unconsciousness at high levels
Management: improve ventilation, remove from exposure
In saturation: check scrubber, ventilate the chamber

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->

CO2 interacts with other gases.
It increases the risk of both nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity.
A diver with elevated CO2 is more vulnerable to narcosis at depth and to an O2 seizure on a treatment table.

03
Carbon Monoxide Poisoning
Sources, signs, and treatment

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Carbon Monoxide
Sources: compressor intake contamination, exhaust fumes, fire
CO binds to haemoglobin 200-250x more strongly than oxygen
Tissue hypoxia despite apparently adequate oxygen delivery
Pulse oximetry reads falsely high (cannot distinguish COHb from O2Hb)
Cherry red skin is classically taught but unreliable and often absent
Signs: headache, nausea, confusion, collapse, cardiac arrest
Treatment: high-flow 100% oxygen, hyperbaric oxygen if available
Prevention: gas testing, compressor intake placement away from exhaust

Other Contaminants, Anoxia, and Narcosis

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Other Breathing Gas Contaminants
Hydrocarbons: oil mist from compressor, diesel fumes from engine exhaust
Effects: headache, nausea, respiratory irritation, narcotic effect
Other contaminants: solvents, cleaning chemicals near gas intakes
Key message: vague symptoms (headache, nausea) may be gas contamination
A high index of suspicion is needed, especially if multiple people are affected
Management: remove from exposure, fresh air or oxygen, supportive care

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Anoxia/Hypoxia and Nitrogen Narcosis

Anoxia and hypoxia
Nitrogen narcosis
Causes: gas supply failure, wrong gas mix, hypoxic mix at surface
Onset can be instant: unconsciousness with no warning
No time for the diver to help themselves
Management: immediate oxygen, rescue
Bottom mix hazard: a deep mix breathed at surface can be hypoxic
Mechanism: lipid solubility of nitrogen at high partial pressure
Onset roughly from 30 msw on air (ppN2 > 3.2 bar)
Signs: impaired judgement, euphoria, anxiety, poor coordination
Individual variation is significant
Management: ascend to reduce ppN2
DMT task: recognise narcosis in someone else, not just yourself

05
Inhalation Injuries
Hot gases, smoke, and toxic fumes

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 17 -->
Inhalation of Hot, Toxic Gases, or Smoke
Airway burns: heat damage to upper airway, swelling may develop over hours
Warning signs: singed facial hair, soot in nostrils or mouth, hoarse voice
Late deterioration: patient may initially seem well then develop airway obstruction
Smoke inhalation: carbon particles cause chemical irritation deep in the lungs
Toxic fumes in confined spaces: common on vessels (cargo holds, tanks)
Chamber fire: extremely hazardous in oxygen-enriched atmosphere
Management: early airway assessment, high-flow oxygen, prepare for deterioration

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->

Integration: Pattern Recognition
Identify the likely gas toxicity and immediate management for each scenario:
Discussion Points
Diver at 60 msw develops facial twitching and confusion (O2 toxicity: switch to air, do not ascend during seizure)
Saturation diver complains of persistent headache over several hours (CO2 retention: check atmosphere, improve ventilation)
Surface diver collapses on deck immediately after surfacing from deep bounce dive (hypoxia or AGE: immediate O2, consider DCI pathway)

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
Key Takeaways
Oxygen toxicity: VENTID-C recognition, convulsion management, do not ascend during seizure
CO2: headache is the early warning. Check scrubbers, check ventilation.
CO: pulse oximetry lies. Treat with high-flow O2. Prevent through gas testing.
Hypoxia: instant unconsciousness, no warning. Immediate rescue and oxygen.
Narcosis: recognise it in others. Ascend to reduce ppN2.
Inhalation injury: watch for late deterioration. Airway first.
