Head Injuries and Stroke

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Head Injuries

Recognition
Management
Mechanism: fall, struck by object, blast
Primary brain injury: at the moment of impact
Secondary brain injury: swelling, bleeding, hypoxia afterwards
Glasgow Coma Scale: Eyes (1-4), Verbal (1-5), Motor (1-6)
GCS 15 = normal. GCS 8 or below = cannot protect own airway.
ABCDE: airway protection is the priority in low GCS
Scalp wounds: profuse bleeding, control with direct pressure
Skull fracture signs: Battle's sign, racoon eyes, CSF leak
Raised ICP: cushing response (hypertension + bradycardia)
Position: head up 30 degrees if no spinal injury suspected
Evacuate: all significant head injuries need hospital assessment

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Stroke and the DCI Differential

FAST recognition
Stroke vs DCI in a diver
F: Face drooping on one side
A: Arm weakness (cannot hold both arms up equally)
S: Speech slurred or absent
T: Time to act (time-critical condition)
Both can present with sudden neurological deficit
A diver who surfaces with neuro symptoms: could be either
The DMT does not need to make the diagnosis
Treat both urgently: oxygen, ABCDE, neuro exam, physician contact
Let the physician decide the treatment pathway

02
Seizures, Epilepsy, and Convulsions

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
Seizure Management
Causes in diving: O2 toxicity, DCI, head injury, epilepsy, hypoglycaemia, febrile illness
Tonic-clonic seizure: stiffening then rhythmic jerking, loss of consciousness
Management: protect from injury, do not restrain, do not put anything in the mouth
Time the seizure. Most resolve spontaneously within 2-3 minutes.
After the seizure: recovery position, maintain airway, oxygen, assess for cause
Status epilepticus: seizure lasting > 5 minutes. Physician guidance for emergency medication.
All seizures in a diver require physician notification and investigation

03
Mental Illness in Diving

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
Recognising and Supporting Mental Health Issues
Stress and anxiety: common offshore. Watch for withdrawal, irritability, sleep disruption.
Panic: acute onset, may occur during diving (dangerous underwater)
Depression: low mood, loss of interest, fatigue, social withdrawal. Risk factors: isolation, shift patterns.
Psychosis: loss of contact with reality. Rare but serious. Safety is the priority.
Saturation factors: confined space, prolonged isolation, limited privacy, interpersonal friction
The DMT's role: recognise, support, communicate with physician. Do not attempt to diagnose.
Do not dismiss. Do not minimise. A colleague in distress deserves to be taken seriously.

Syncope and Crush Injuries

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 12 -->
Syncope and Crush Injuries

Syncope (fainting)
Crush injuries
Vasovagal: most common. Standing, heat, pain, stress.
Postural: standing up too quickly after lying down
Cardiac: arrhythmia, valvular disease (more sinister)
Dehydration: common offshore in hot environments
Management: lie flat, legs elevated, monitor, identify cause
If not recovering or if cardiac cause suspected: physician
Mechanism: limb trapped under heavy object for prolonged time
Complications: rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, renal failure
Releasing a prolonged crush can cause cardiac arrest
Hyperkalaemia from muscle breakdown stops the heart
Get physician guidance BEFORE releasing the crush
IV fluids (large volumes) before and during release

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->

Crush injury: do not release the limb without preparation.
Releasing without IV fluids and monitoring can kill the patient.
This is counterintuitive. Your instinct is to free the trapped person immediately. Resist that instinct and call the physician first.

Minor Illnesses, Dental, and Marine Animals

### Notes:

Common Minor Illnesses

Common presentations
The saturation challenge
Seasickness: prevention (medication) and treatment
Skin conditions: dermatitis, fungal infections in saturation
GI problems: diarrhoea, constipation (diet and medication)
Respiratory: common cold, fitness to dive implications
UTI: recognition, when to treat (physician guidance)
A minor illness on shore is a significant problem in saturation
Patient cannot be easily removed from the system
Extended self-care under physician guidance may be needed
Prevention is better than treatment: hygiene protocols
Dental problems: pain relief, temp filling material, antibiotics

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 16 -->
Dangerous Marine Animals
Jellyfish stings: remove tentacles (do not rub), vinegar for box jellyfish, hot water for others
Fish spine injuries (weeverfish, stonefish, lionfish): hot water immersion (as hot as bearable)
Coral cuts: clean thoroughly, tend to become infected easily
Severe envenomation: watch for anaphylaxis. Treat as per anaphylaxis algorithm if signs develop.
Management principles are more useful than species identification
Prevention: awareness, protective clothing, local knowledge of hazards

HPNS, Compression Arthralgia, and Blast Injury

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 18 -->
Deep Diving and Blast Conditions
HPNS (High Pressure Nervous Syndrome): tremor, nausea, reduced performance at deep depths (>150 msw)
Caused by helium effects on the nervous system. Managed by slowing compression rate.
Compression arthralgia: joint pain during compression. Usually self-limiting. Simple analgesia.
Underwater blast injury: rare but serious. Mechanism: pressure wave through water.
Primary blast injuries: gut (most vulnerable), lungs, ears
Management: ABCDE, suspect internal injuries even without external wounds
The DMT needs to recognise these conditions exist and communicate with the physician

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
Diving Accidents: The DMT Role (Brief Overview)
First response priorities: scene safety, casualty care, communication
Communication chain: DMT to supervisor to physician to shore-based support
Do not delay treatment while establishing communications
Incident reporting: factual, contemporaneous, what happened and when
Preservation of evidence: do not discard equipment, do not clean the scene
The refresher module RM7 covers incident review in depth with case studies

Key Takeaways
Head injuries and stroke: ABCDE, GCS, protect the airway, evacuate
Stroke vs DCI: both present with sudden neuro deficit in a diver. Treat both urgently.
Seizures: protect, do not restrain, time it, recovery position after, find the cause
Crush injuries: do not release without preparation. Physician guidance first.
Mental health: recognise, support, communicate. Do not dismiss or diagnose.
The unifying theme: recognise, stabilise, communicate, get physician input
