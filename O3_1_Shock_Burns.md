Shock is a failure of tissue perfusion.
Every organ needs blood flow to survive. When that fails, the patient dies.
Your job: recognise it, treat the cause where you can, support the circulation, and get physician input.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 5 -->
Hypovolaemic and Cardiogenic Shock

Hypovolaemic (volume loss)
Cardiogenic (pump failure)
Blood loss: trauma, internal bleeding
Fluid loss: burns, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration
Signs: tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, cold peripheries
Compensated: body maintains BP through vasoconstriction
Decompensated: BP crashes, confusion, collapse
Heart cannot pump effectively
Causes: MI, arrhythmia, cardiac tamponade
Signs: similar to hypovolaemic PLUS pulmonary oedema
Breathlessness even when lying flat
IV fluids may make this worse (unlike hypovolaemic shock)
Physician guidance essential for fluid management

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 6 -->
Neurogenic, Septic, and Anaphylactic Shock

Neurogenic and septic
Anaphylactic
Neurogenic: spinal cord injury causes vasodilation
Signs: hypotension WITH bradycardia (not tachycardia)
Warm, flushed skin below the level of injury
Septic: overwhelming infection, late presentation
Signs: fever (or hypothermia), tachycardia, confusion
Both require physician-directed management
Severe allergic reaction: rapid onset, potentially fatal
Causes offshore: marine stings, drugs, latex, food
Airway swelling, bronchospasm, urticaria, hypotension
Treatment: IM adrenaline first, then oxygen and fluids
Can deteriorate extremely rapidly
Covered in detail on the next slides

Anaphylaxis
Recognition and the management algorithm

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 8 -->
Recognising Anaphylaxis
Rapid onset after exposure to a trigger (usually minutes)
Airway: swelling of tongue, lips, throat. Stridor. Hoarse voice.
Breathing: bronchospasm, wheeze, respiratory distress
Circulation: tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, dizziness, collapse
Skin: urticaria (hives), flushing, angioedema
Gut: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
Does not require all features to be present. Airway OR circulation compromise with a plausible trigger = treat.

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
Anaphylaxis Management Algorithm
1. Remove the trigger if possible (stop the drug, remove the sting)
2. IM adrenaline: 0.5 mg (0.5 ml of 1:1000) into the outer thigh
3. Lie the patient flat with legs elevated (if breathing allows)
4. High-flow oxygen via reservoir mask
5. IV access and fluid challenge (under physician guidance)
6. Repeat adrenaline at 5-minute intervals if no improvement
7. Second-line: antihistamines and hydrocortisone (physician direction)
8. Monitor for recurrence: biphasic reaction can occur hours later
Practical IM injection technique is taught in-person (Day 2)

Electric Shock

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 11 -->
Electric Shock: Recognition and Management
Offshore sources: welding equipment, power tools, damaged cables, switchboards
Mechanisms of injury: electrical burn (entry and exit), cardiac arrhythmia, muscle spasm, falls
Safety first: isolate the power source before touching the casualty
Management: ABCDE approach, cardiac monitoring (arrhythmia risk persists for hours)
Entry and exit wounds may look small but deep tissue damage can be extensive
Compartment syndrome risk in affected limbs
Hyperbaric considerations: electrical equipment in chambers must be certified safe
Chamber fire risk from electrical faults in oxygen-enriched atmosphere

Burns
Assessment, management, and hyperbaric considerations

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->
Burns Assessment

Depth classification
Extent estimation
Superficial (epidermal): red, painful, no blisters. Like sunburn.
Partial thickness (dermal): blisters, very painful, moist surface
Full thickness: white or charred, painless (nerves destroyed), dry
Mixed depth burns are common
Rule of Nines: head 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%
Palm method: patient's palm (including fingers) = approximately 1% BSA
Lund-Browder chart: more accurate but needs the chart available
Special areas: face, hands, perineum, circumferential burns
These always need physician involvement

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 14 -->
Burns Management
Cooling: cool running water for 20 minutes (within 3 hours of injury)
Do not use ice. Do not overcool: hypothermia is a risk in extensive burns
Covering: cling film (laid on, not wrapped circumferentially), non-adherent dressings
Pain: significant. Paracetamol and ibuprofen for minor burns. Morphine may be needed (physician guidance).
Fluid resuscitation: burns >15% BSA in adults need IV fluids
Parkland formula (simplified): 4 ml x body weight (kg) x %BSA. Half in first 8 hours.
The DMT does not need to calculate Parkland precisely. Understand the principle: big burns need big volumes.
Hyperbaric considerations: dressing in confined space, oxygen effects on wound healing

### Notes:

<!-- Slide number: 15 -->
Associated Injuries
Inhalation injury: singed facial hair, soot in airway, hoarse voice (cross-reference O2.1)
Blast injury: burns plus blunt trauma, eardrums, blast lung
Circumferential burns: limb or chest. Risk of vascular compromise or respiratory restriction.
Chemical burns: specific decontamination protocols depending on agent
Flash fire on deck: common offshore scenario. Multiple casualties possible.

Case 2: Burns

Key Takeaways
Shock: recognise the signs (tachycardia, hypotension, confusion), treat the cause, support the circulation
Anaphylaxis: IM adrenaline 0.5 mg into the outer thigh. Do not delay. Repeat at 5 minutes if needed.
Electric shock: isolate power first. Cardiac monitoring. Look for entry and exit wounds.
Burns: cool for 20 minutes, cover with cling film, assess depth and extent, IV fluids for >15% BSA
For all of these: ABCDE, physician contact, and documentation using DMAC 01
